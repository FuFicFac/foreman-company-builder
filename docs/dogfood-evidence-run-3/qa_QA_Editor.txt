# Project Brief: Short-Story Commission

## Title
**The Tuesday Ledger**

## Premise
In a mid-size industrial city, a municipal records clerk discovers that three consecutive “accidental” water-main breaks share a signature only someone who files the work orders would notice. When the pattern points at her own department—and at the colleague who trained her—she has one week of paper trail before the archive is digitized and the originals are shredded. The story is not a thriller chase; it is about what a careful person does with proof she never asked to hold.

## Characters
- **Elena Voss** — Protagonist; 34; assistant clerk in the Department of Public Works records office; meticulous, risk-averse, recently promoted past a senior clerk who expected the job.
- **Ray Okonkwo** — Senior clerk who mentored Elena for six years; genial, indispensable on the floor, the person whose initials appear on the “routine” re-filings that don’t match the field logs.
- **Captain June Park** — City inspector who signed the final safety clearances after each break; appears only in documents and one brief in-person exchange; not a villain cartoon, but her signature is the hinge Elena cannot ignore.

## Timeline
- **March 3, 2025 (Monday)** — First water-main break: Willow & 12th. Official cause: “age of pipe.” Elena files the incident packet same day; notices the repair crew’s timesheet was logged *before* the break call-in time, but shrugs it off as a clock error.
- **March 17, 2025 (Monday)** — Second break: Mercer Industrial Spur. Same “age of pipe” language; same pre-stamped timesheet pattern. Elena begins a private notebook (paper only).
- **March 31, 2025 (Monday)** — Third break: under the loading dock of the city warehouse that stores surplus copper fittings. Elena matches three work-order numbers to a single re-file batch Ray initialed on **April 1**.
- **April 1–4, 2025** — Digitization vendor on-site; originals marked for shredding after scan. Elena has access to the pre-shred room only during her shifts: **8:00 a.m.–4:30 p.m.**, Monday–Friday.
- **April 7, 2025 (Monday), 3:55 p.m.** — Story’s present-day climax window: last chance to pull the three physical packets before the vendor seals the bin. Elena’s shift ends at 4:30; the shred truck is scheduled for **4:15 p.m.**
- Continuity rules for the writer: all three breaks land on Mondays exactly two weeks apart; the re-file batch date is the day *after* the third break; Elena’s notebook entries must not invent events outside this span; no “months later” epilogue.

## Required beats
1. Open in the records office on **April 7** with Elena mid-routine, then flash back only as needed—and only within the March 3–April 7 window—to establish how she noticed the pattern.
2. Show the documentary evidence on the page (timesheets, re-file initials, Park’s clearances) so the reader can follow without trusting Elena’s paranoia alone.
3. A live friction scene with Ray (not a confession dump): he is helpful, almost kind, and that makes the choice harder.
4. One concrete obstacle to removing the files (badge log, co-worker present, vendor early, locked cart—pick one and make it physical).
5. End on Elena’s choice in the room, before 4:15, without a narrator verdict on whether she was right.

## Constraints
- **Length:** 600–900 words.
- **Voice:** Third person, past tense.
- **POV:** Single — Elena only. No head-hopping; Ray and Park only as Elena perceives them.
- No lighthouse, lamp, or characters named Theo or Mara.
- No omniscient reveal of a grand conspiracy past what Elena can prove from paper and one conversation.
- Keep municipal detail specific enough to feel real; avoid procedural lecture.

## Ending
The story must close on a **concrete choice Elena makes on the page** at or just before the shred deadline—e.g., she either walks the three packets out under her coat, feeds them into the shredder herself, slides them into Ray’s pending tray, or hands them to Park in the lobby. The last beat is the action of choosing, not the long-term consequence.
# The Tuesday Ledger

At 3:41 on the seventh of April, Elena Voss was still initialing the day's intake, because a person who stops initialing is a person who has been noticed. The records office still smelled of toner and the vendor's ozone, though the scanners had been crated out the previous Friday when the last originals went through. Two rows over, the scanned packets waited in a gray bin marked SHRED — 4:15. Her shift ended at 4:30. She had done the arithmetic on that gap for a week.

The pattern had not announced itself. On March 3, the Willow & 12th main had burst, official cause "age of pipe," and she had filed the incident packet the same day. The repair crew's timesheet was stamped in before the break was even called in. A clock error, she'd told herself, the way you tell yourself weather.

Then March 17, the Mercer Industrial Spur — same "age of pipe," same timesheet stamped ahead of the call. That night she'd bought a paper notebook, cash, and started writing by hand, because paper does not keep a log of who opened it.

March 31 was the one she couldn't shrug. The break came up under the loading dock of the warehouse where the city stored surplus copper fittings — the exact place a person would want a flooded, condemned floor and an insurance clock. She'd pulled the three work-order numbers and traced them to a single re-file batch, re-stamped as one lot on April 1, the day after. The initials on the batch slip were RO, in the small tidy hand she had spent six years learning to read.

Ray Okonkwo brought her coffee at 3:48, the way he had every afternoon since she was new and terrified of the microfilm reader.

"You're deep in it," he said, nodding at her screen. "They finished the last of the scans Friday. Truck comes for the originals at quarter past. Feels like burning the library, doesn't it."

"Feels like something," she said.

He leaned on the partition, genial, indispensable, the man who'd covered her first week's mistakes without ever mentioning them. "Anything you can't find, ask me before it's a JPEG. Paper remembers where it's been." He tapped the intake tray. "Whole life in here, Elena. Ours too." He said it lightly. It landed like a hand on her shoulder, and that was the trouble: kindness was not a clue, and she could not file it as one.

He went back to the floor. She looked at the notebook in her drawer and the three physical packets she'd flagged that morning — Willow, Mercer, the warehouse — sitting in the pre-shred cart against the wall.

At 4:02 she crossed to the cart. The vendor had run a plastic zip-tie through the handle and clipped a green tag: SEALED FOR TRANSFER. Not locked. Sealed. A cut tie was a thing a badge log could ask about; the pre-shred room logged every entry against a name, and hers was the name on the door for this shift. Any packet that left would leave a shape in the record exactly where she was standing.

She had Captain June Park's clearances in the folder too — the safety sign-offs that closed each incident, one crisp signature after every break, the hinge she could not pretend away. Park had passed her once in the lobby and said the elevator was slow today, and that was the whole of what Elena knew of her that wasn't ink.

4:09. The vendor's porter wheeled an empty hand-truck toward the freight elevator and left the row unwatched. Down the hall the shred truck idled; she could hear it through the loading bay, six minutes early or on time depending on whose clock.

Elena took the small folding knife from her drawer.

She thought about the coat on the back of her chair, wide enough to hide three packets. She thought about Ray's pending tray, where a returned batch would sit unremarkable until Monday, back in the tidy hands that had re-filed it once already. She thought about the lobby, and Park, and the slow elevator.

She cut the tie.

The three packets came out light, lighter than a week of not sleeping had made them feel. The week's scans had already settled onto a drive Ray could open from the floor — the same archive that had swallowed the re-file batch and stamped it as one lot the day after. A scan could be renamed. A scan could be re-filed in that small tidy hand; she had watched it done. Paper could not be initialed twice, and the packets in her arms were the only Willow, Mercer, and warehouse originals his initials could never be lifted from. She squared their edges the way she squared everything, folded her coat around them, and at 4:11 by the wall clock Elena Voss walked — not toward the shredder, not toward Ray's pending tray, but toward the door and the badge reader that would write her name against the hour, three packets of paper held to her chest and carried out of every system the vendor's scan could reach.
